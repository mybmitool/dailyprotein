The Protein Deficient Vegan Who Benched 225

2026-07-16 · 5 min read · General
General

The Protein-Deficient Vegan Who Benched 225: A Client Story

Listen, I have heard every joke about vegan protein. I have heard "where do you get your protein" more times than I have heard my own name. And I have seen the data — real data, from real studies — that shows plant-based athletes can build just as much muscle as meat-eaters if they do it right. But doing it right is the hard part. And Miguel was not doing it right.

He came to me four weeks out from his first natural bodybuilding show in San Diego. He was panicking. Seven years vegan, ethical reasons, not health. He had been crushing his training. His conditioning was on point. His posing needed work, but that is true for every first-timer. The problem was his protein.

He was eating 180 grams of protein per day. That should have been enough. He was 185 pounds, aiming for the stage at 168. At 180 grams, he was hitting roughly 1 gram per pound of target body weight. By the textbook, he was fine. But the textbook does not account for bioavailability. And Miguel's protein sources were mostly rice, beans, and a commercial plant-based powder that had the PDCAAS score of a potato.

I ran his numbers. His actual absorbable protein — accounting for digestibility and amino acid completeness — was closer to 120 grams. That is a 60-gram deficit. In a deficit phase, when muscle preservation is everything, that is catastrophic.

His leucine was the real problem. Muscle protein synthesis requires roughly 2.5 to 3 grams of leucine per meal to trigger the mTOR pathway. Miguel's meals were averaging 1.8 grams. He was eating frequently — six meals a day — but none of them were hitting the leucine threshold. It is like ringing a doorbell with half the voltage required. You are making noise, but nobody is answering the door.

I sat him down in my apartment — the one I share with my roommate, who is also a trainer and who was eating a rotisserie chicken in the background while we talked. I told Miguel the truth: he needed to change his protein strategy, or he was going to lose muscle on stage. And losing muscle on stage is the fastest way to look flat, small, and like you do not belong there.

We rebuilt his plan in one afternoon. First, we swapped his protein powder. The one he was using was mostly rice protein with some pea protein blended in. Rice protein is low in lysine. Pea protein is low in methionine. Together they are better than either alone, but they still fall short of whey in leucine content. I found him a blend that added pumpkin seed protein and sunflower seed protein — higher leucine, better amino acid spread. It cost more. He did not care. He was four weeks out.

Second, we added whole food sources that actually deliver leucine. Tofu, tempeh, seitan, and — this is the one that surprised him — vital wheat gluten. Seitan is roughly 75% protein by calories, and it is dense in leucine. A 6-ounce serving of seitan delivers about 3.2 grams of leucine. That is more than chicken breast. I showed him the data from the USDA FoodData Central. He stared at it for a long time. "I have been avoiding gluten," he said. I said, "Unless you have celiac disease, gluten is just protein. And right now, you need protein."

Third, we restructured his meals. Instead of six small meals with inadequate leucine, we moved to four larger meals. Breakfast was tofu scramble with nutritional yeast — 35 grams of protein, 2.8 grams of leucine. Lunch was tempeh stir-fry — 40 grams of protein, 3.1 grams of leucine. Dinner was seitan with vegetables — 45 grams of protein, 3.4 grams of leucine. And his post-workout shake was the new blend — 30 grams of protein, 2.9 grams of leucine. Every meal hit the threshold. Every meal triggered synthesis.

His roommate thought he was crazy. His gym buddies asked if he was "still vegan." He said yes. He said it louder than necessary. I understood. When you are doing something hard and countercultural, you get defensive. But the data was on his side.

At two weeks out, his strength was holding. He was still benching 225 for reps. His squat was down slightly — 315 to 295 — but that was expected in a deficit. His physique looked fuller. His shoulders had the roundness that comes from preserved muscle mass. His coach — a guy named Steve who has been in the industry for twenty years — pulled me aside at the gym and said, "Whatever you did, keep doing it." I told him it was just math. He said, "Math is underrated."

Show day came. Miguel stepped on stage at 167.4 pounds, shredded, with muscle fullness that surprised the judges. He placed third in his class — not first, but for a first-timer in a natural show, third is a victory. And he did it without eating a single animal product. He proved what the literature has been saying for years: vegan athletes can compete at the highest levels if they understand protein quality, leucine thresholds, and meal distribution.

After the show, he took me to a vegan restaurant in North Park. He ordered a seitan steak and ate the whole thing while telling me about his plans for the next show. He wants to win. I think he can. Not because he is special — though he is dedicated. Because he now has the data. And data, when applied correctly, is the great equalizer.

I still eat chicken breast. I still have my Sunday ice cream. I am not vegan. But Miguel taught me something I will not forget: the limits we place on plant-based nutrition are often based on ignorance, not evidence. The evidence says you can build muscle on plants. You just have to be smarter about it. And being smarter is what I do.

— Dex, from San Diego, where the sun is hot and the protein math is finally honest.
