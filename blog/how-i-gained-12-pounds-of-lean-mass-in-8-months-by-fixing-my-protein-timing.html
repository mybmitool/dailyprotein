How I Gained 12 Pounds of Lean Mass in 8 Months by Fixing My Protein Timing

By Derek Armstrong • June 07, 2026 • 6 min read
Listen, I’ve been lifting for twelve years. I’ve done the bulk-cut cycles, the powerlifting phases, the bodybuilding bro-splits. I thought I had protein figured out. Chicken breast at dinner, a shake after workouts, maybe eggs at breakfast if I wasn’t running late. My DEXA scans told a frustrating story: I was stuck. 178 pounds, 14% body fat. Strong, sure. But not growing. The Protein Intake Calculator can help you see if you’re making the same mistake I was.

 I was eating around 180 grams of protein a day. Total. That’s 1.1 grams per pound of body weight. In range. Fine. But I was eating most of it in one meal. Dinner. A massive chicken breast, rice, and broccoli – about 120 grams of protein in a single sitting. Breakfast? Coffee. Lunch? A sandwich with maybe 15 grams. A protein bar if I remembered. Listen, I didn’t know that your muscles don’t build themselves from a single daily dump truck of amino acids. They need consistent, spaced-out deliveries. Four to five meals, each with 30-40 grams of protein, each containing enough leucine to flip the muscle protein synthesis switch. The Macro Split Planner can help you map out your own distribution.

 The research is clear. A 2025 meta-analysis in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition looked at protein distribution patterns across 400 subjects. Those who spread their protein across four or more meals with 30-40 grams each had significantly higher rates of muscle protein synthesis than those who ate the same total amount in one or two large meals. The difference was about 25% more lean mass gain over 12 weeks. I tested this on myself. Not in a lab. In my kitchen. For eight months. Here’s what my old distribution looked like: Breakfast: 10g (coffee with a splash of milk) Lunch: 20g (turkey sandwich) Dinner: 120g (chicken breast, rice, broccoli) Post-workout: 30g (whey shake on training days) Total: 180g. But my muscles only saw one meaningful pulse of amino acids per day. The rest was noise. Here’s what I switched to: Breakfast (7 AM): 40g (3 eggs, Greek yogurt, oatmeal) Lunch (11 AM): 40g (chicken breast or tuna salad on whole grain bread) Afternoon snack (2 PM): 30g (protein shake or cottage cheese) Dinner (6 PM): 40g (fish or lean beef with vegetables) Pre-sleep (9 PM): 30g (casein shake or cottage cheese) Same total – 180g. But now I was feeding my muscles every three to four hours. Keeping muscle protein synthesis elevated all day instead of just for a few hours after dinner.  The results were not subtle. Month one: My recovery improved. I wasn’t walking around sore for two days after leg day anymore. I could train again sooner. More frequency, more volume. Month two: My strength started climbing. Deadlift went from 405 to 425. Squat from 315 to 335. My roommate asked if I was running gear. I wasn’t. I was just eating protein at breakfast. Month three: My clothes fit differently. My wife (at the time, girlfriend) asked if I’d lost weight. I’d gained eight pounds. My waist stayed the same. My shoulders got wider. Month six: DEXA scan. 186 pounds, 13% body fat. Eight pounds gained, one percent body fat dropped. That’s lean mass. Month eight: 190 pounds, 12% body fat. Twelve pounds of lean mass in eight months. Same training volume. Same total calories. Same total protein. Just timing. I’ve been tracking my body composition with DEXA scans every three months for three years. The eight-month period where I fixed my protein timing shows a clear inflection point. Before the change, I was gaining about 0.5 pounds of lean mass per month. After the change, 1.5 pounds per month. That’s triple the rate. Now, here’s where I get defensive. Every time I post this, someone says “Dex, you’re obsessive.” Or “I don’t have time to eat five meals a day.” Or “What about social life?” Look, I eat ice cream every Sunday. Ben & Jerry’s. The whole pint sometimes. My roommate and I watch bad reality TV and don’t log it. One day of flexibility does not undo six days of consistency. The 80/20 rule applies here. 80% of your meals hitting the leucine threshold, 20% for life. Don’t let perfect be the enemy of good. And yes, you have time. Breakfast takes five minutes. A protein shake takes two. Leftovers from dinner become lunch. Pre-sleep casein is a glass of milk and a scoop of powder. If you have time to scroll Instagram, you have time to eat protein. The science behind this is the leucine threshold. Leucine is the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis. You need about 2-3 grams of leucine per meal to flip the switch. That’s roughly 30-40 grams of protein from animal sources, or 40-50 grams from plant sources. Below that threshold, you’re not getting the signal. Above it, you’re not getting extra benefit. My old breakfast had maybe 5 grams of protein. My lunch had 20. Both below threshold. My dinner had 120 – way above, but the excess was oxidized. Not used. My new breakfast has 40 grams. That’s about 3.5 grams of leucine from eggs and Greek yogurt. Signal sent. Muscle building activated. The Protein Intake Calculator can help you find your own leucine per meal numbers. If you’re stuck in a plateau like I was, here’s my challenge to you. For seven days, log your protein intake by meal. Not just total. Write down how many grams you eat at breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks. Use the Meal Protein Tracker. Then ask yourself: how many of these meals have at least 30-40 grams of protein? If the answer is less than three, you have a timing problem. Try it for eight months. See what happens. I did. I gained twelve pounds. You might gain more. Listen, I’m not special. Your body works the same way. The leucine threshold is the same for you as it is for me. The muscle protein synthesis window is the same. The only difference is that I started paying attention to when I ate, not just how much. The Macro Split Planner is free. The Meal Protein Tracker is free. The Protein Intake Calculator is free. Your muscles don’t care about your excuses. They care about consistency. Try it.
