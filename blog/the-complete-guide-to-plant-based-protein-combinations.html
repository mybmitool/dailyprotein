The Complete Guide to Plant-Based Protein Combinations

By Derek Armstrong • June 12, 2026 • 6 min read
My client Miguel wasn't the only vegan who struggled. After his competition prep, I started getting calls from plant-based athletes across the country. Powerlifters, ultrarunners, CrossFitters, yoga instructors. All of them had the same problem. They were eating plants. They were training hard. They weren't getting the results they expected.  Here's the truth about plant protein. It's not that plants don't have enough protein. Lentils have 18 grams per cup. Tofu has 20 grams per half block. Seitan has 25 grams per serving. Chickpeas have 15 grams per cup. The problem is amino acid distribution. The Vegan Protein Adequacy Calculator can help you figure out if you're getting what you need.

 Most plant proteins are incomplete. That means they're missing one or more of the nine essential amino acids. Grains like rice and wheat are low in lysine. Legumes like beans and lentils are low in methionine. If you eat only rice, you miss lysine. If you eat only beans, you miss methionine. But if you eat rice and beans together, you get both. That's complementary protein pairing. The concept isn't new. Vegetarians have known about it for decades. But bodybuilders and athletes have been slow to adopt it. Let me give you the top five complementary pairs. Rice and beans. The classic. White rice, brown rice, wild rice – any grain works with any legume. Black beans, pinto beans, kidney beans, lentils. The ratio isn't critical. Just eat them together in the same meal. A burrito bowl with rice and black beans. Rice and lentil soup. A grain bowl with chickpeas. Pea and rice protein powder. This is the supplement version. Pea protein is high in lysine, low in methionine. Rice protein is the opposite. Together, they form a complete profile. Most high-quality vegan protein powders use this blend. Hummus and whole wheat pita. Hummus is made from chickpeas (legume). Pita is made from wheat (grain). Together, complete protein. Same with peanut butter on whole wheat bread. Not fancy, but effective. Tofu and broccoli. Tofu is soy, which is already a complete protein – one of the few plant sources that is. But adding broccoli boosts the profile and adds fiber, vitamins, and minerals. Oatmeal and pumpkin seeds. Oats are a grain, low in lysine. Pumpkin seeds are high in lysine. Add seeds to your morning oatmeal. Complete protein. The Protein Source Comparator can help you rank different plant proteins by their amino acid scores.

 Why leucine matters more for vegans Remember the leucine threshold? 2-3 grams per meal to trigger muscle protein synthesis. Animal proteins hit that easily. Whey has 11% leucine. Chicken has 8%. Beef has 8%. Eggs have 8.5%. That means 30 grams of whey gives you 3.3 grams of leucine. 30 grams of chicken gives you 2.4 grams. Right at the threshold. Plant proteins have less leucine per gram. Soy has about 8% – similar to chicken. Pea has about 7%. Rice has about 5%. Lentils have about 6%. To get 2.5 grams of leucine from lentils, you need about 40 grams of protein. That's a lot of lentils. A cup of cooked lentils has 18 grams of protein. You'd need two cups. That's 400 calories just from lentils. That's why vegans need to eat more total protein than omnivores, not less. The leucine threshold is harder to hit with plants. Practical tips for hitting your leucine target First, use complementary pairs in every meal. Not once a day. Every meal. Breakfast, lunch, dinner, snacks. Second, consider a vegan protein powder with a high leucine content. Pea-rice blends are good. Soy isolate is also good. Add it to oatmeal, smoothies, or drink it alone. Third, eat more. If you're a 180-pound vegan athlete, you might need 200-220 grams of protein per day, not 160. That's the reality of lower leucine density. Sample vegan day hitting 180 grams Breakfast: Oatmeal (10g) + soy milk (8g) + pumpkin seeds (10g) + protein powder (25g) = 53g Lunch: Tofu stir-fry (30g) + brown rice (5g) + edamame (15g) = 50g Afternoon snack: Protein shake (25g) + peanut butter (8g) = 33g Dinner: Seitan (30g) + lentil soup (15g) + whole wheat bread (5g) = 50g Pre-sleep: Soy yogurt (10g) = 10g Total: 196 grams. That's doable. The B12, iron, and zinc problem Listen, I have to mention this because too many vegans ignore it. Plant protein is great. But plant-based diets are often low in vitamin B12, iron, and zinc. B12 deficiency causes fatigue, brain fog, and nerve damage. Iron deficiency causes anemia. Zinc deficiency impairs immune function. Get blood work every year. Supplement B12. Eat iron-rich plants with vitamin C to increase absorption. Think spinach with lemon juice, lentils with bell peppers. Fortified foods help. The Vegan Protein Adequacy Calculator includes cross-checks for these nutrients. Real client success story A client named Sarah, 28 years old, vegan for five years, came to me stuck at 135 pounds. She was a CrossFitter. She was strong but couldn't put on mass. Her protein intake was 90 grams a day. She was eating oats for breakfast, a salad for lunch, tofu for dinner. Not enough. Not even close. We bumped her to 150 grams using the strategies above. Pea-rice protein shake after training. Tempeh instead of tofu (more protein). Lentils added to every meal. Within four months, she gained 8 pounds of lean mass. Her deadlift went from 225 to 255. She told me “I was so scared of eating too much. I was eating like a 120-pound sedentary woman while training like a 180-pound athlete.” That's the vegan trap. Under-eating because of volume. Plants are bulky. They fill your stomach. You feel full before you've hit your protein target. You have to push through that. The bottom line Plant-based athletes can build muscle. They can compete. They can thrive. But they need to be intentional. Track your protein. Aim for the higher end of the range (2.0-2.2 g/kg). Use complementary pairs. Consider a vegan protein powder. Get blood work. Supplement B12.
