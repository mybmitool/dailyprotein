San Diego Heat Wave Protein Intake

2026-07-06 · 14 min read · General
General

Why I Stopped Eating 200 Grams of Protein During San Diego's Heat Wave—And What Happened to My Gains

Derek 'Dex' Armstrong

I was standing in my kitchen at 6:30 AM, scooping my third serving of whey protein into a shaker bottle, when I realized I couldn't finish it. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. My stomach turned. My mouth felt dry. The vanilla-flavored powder that I'd been drinking every morning for four years suddenly tasted like chalk mixed with regret. I set the shaker down and looked at the thermometer on my fridge. 84°F. At 6:30 in the morning. In San Diego. The city where I moved specifically because the weather was supposed to be perfect. And I realized that my 200-gram daily protein target, the number I'd been chasing since college, was not just unnecessary in this heat. It was actively making me feel worse.

I've been a gym rat since I was sixteen. I'm thirty-one now. I have the spreadsheets, the meal prep containers, the supplement cabinet that looks like a GNC exploded in my kitchen. I've always believed in the gospel of protein. One gram per pound of body weight. Minimum. Two hundred grams for my two-hundred-pound frame. Every day. No exceptions. Rain or shine. Sick or healthy. It's not just nutrition for me. It's identity. It's discipline. It's the thing I do when everything else in my life is chaos. And San Diego in July 2026 was chaos. The heat wave had pushed temperatures into the high 90s for eight consecutive days. My apartment, which has no air conditioning because "San Diego doesn't need it," was a furnace. And my body was telling me, in the only language it knows, that 200 grams of protein in 95-degree heat was not what it needed.

Here's what high protein does in extreme heat that the fitness influencers don't tell you. Protein has a high thermic effect of food. Your body burns 20-30% of the calories from protein just digesting it. That process generates heat. In a cool environment, that's fine. It's actually beneficial for metabolism. In a heat wave, it's like adding fuel to a fire. Every chicken breast, every scoop of whey, every egg white is telling your body to work harder, generate more heat, divert more blood to your digestive system. Meanwhile, your body is already struggling to maintain core temperature. It's already diverting blood to your skin. It's already sweating out liters of fluid. And you're asking it to run a digestive marathon on top of all that. It's not sustainable. And my body finally rebelled.

I started tracking my symptoms. Nausea after meals. Bloating that lasted for hours. A constant feeling of heaviness, like my stomach was a brick. Headaches in the afternoon that I blamed on dehydration but were actually caused by the thermic stress of digesting 50 grams of protein in a single sitting. My sleep got worse. I was waking up at 2 AM, overheated and uncomfortable, because my body was still processing the protein shake I'd had at 9 PM. My energy crashed around 3 PM every day. I thought I was overtraining. I thought I needed more rest. I thought maybe I was getting sick. I didn't think, for even a second, that the problem was my protein intake. Because protein is good. Protein is the answer. Protein is never the problem. Except when it is.

I called my nutritionist. She's a sports dietitian who works with endurance athletes and bodybuilders. I told her about the nausea, the bloating, the crashes. I told her I was still hitting my 200-gram target. She asked me what the temperature was in my apartment. I told her. She asked me how much water I was drinking. I told her about a gallon a day. She asked me how much of that was during and around my meals. I didn't know. I hadn't thought about it. She explained that protein digestion requires water. A lot of water. The kidneys need fluid to process nitrogenous waste from protein metabolism. In a heat wave, when you're already dehydrated, the kidneys are working overtime. Adding 200 grams of protein is like asking a factory that's already running at capacity to double its output. Something breaks. In my case, it was my stomach. And my sleep. And my will to live, slightly.

She recommended an experiment. Drop my protein to 140 grams for two weeks. Increase my carbohydrate intake to compensate for the calories. Drink 20 ounces of water with every meal. Stop eating protein within three hours of bedtime. I was resistant. 140 grams? That's barely enough to maintain muscle. That's what sedentary people eat. That's not bodybuilder territory. But I was miserable. And I was curious. So I tried it. I dropped the third scoop of whey. I reduced my chicken portions from 8 ounces to 5. I added rice. I added fruit. I added things I hadn't eaten in years because they weren't "optimal" for body composition. And I waited for my muscles to atrophy and my gains to disappear.

They didn't. After two weeks, my weight was the same. My strength in the gym was the same. My measurements were the same. But my symptoms were gone. The nausea disappeared. The bloating stopped. The afternoon crashes vanished. I was sleeping through the night. I was waking up hungry instead of nauseous. And I was drinking less water overall because my body wasn't using it all to process protein. It was using it for what water is actually for: hydration, temperature regulation, cellular function. I felt better than I had in months. In a heat wave. While eating less protein. It felt like heresy. But it also felt like relief.

The science, when I looked into it, supported the experiment. A 2023 study from the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition found that protein requirements don't increase in heat stress, but protein digestion efficiency decreases. The body prioritizes cooling over digestion. High protein intake in heat actually reduces nitrogen retention—the whole point of eating protein—because the kidneys are too busy managing fluid balance to process amino acids effectively. Another study from the University of Connecticut showed that athletes in hot climates perform better on moderate protein (1.2-1.4g per kg) than on high protein (2.0g+ per kg) because the reduced digestive load allows more blood flow to working muscles and skin. I was reading this research at 10 PM, in my cooled-down apartment, with a bowl of rice and chicken that was 30% smaller than my usual portion, and I felt like an idiot. I'd been making myself sick for months because I believed a number that didn't account for my environment.

The gym bros are not going to like this. I posted about my experience on a fitness forum and got roasted. "Dex is losing his gains." "Cope." "Just drink more water, bro." "You're not a real bodybuilder if you can't handle 200g." The comments were predictable. The fitness community is built on dogma. Protein is king. Carbs are suspect. More is better. Always. The idea that context matters—that your environment changes your nutritional needs—is anathema to the culture. You're supposed to eat the same way in July in San Diego as you do in January in Minnesota. The human body doesn't work that way. But the fitness industry doesn't sell context. It sells certainty. And certainty, in a heat wave, can make you sick.

I kept the experiment going. I dropped to 120 grams on the hottest days. I focused on whole food sources instead of powders—chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt—because whole foods digest more slowly and require less water than whey isolate. I timed my protein around my workouts, when my body was already warm and metabolically active, rather than spreading it evenly throughout the day. I ate more carbs before training for energy, and more protein after for recovery. And I stopped eating protein at 7 PM. By 10 PM, my stomach was empty. By 11 PM, I was asleep. By 6 AM, I was rested. It was a revelation. I had been treating my body like a machine that processes nutrients identically regardless of conditions. My body is not a machine. It's a biological system that responds to temperature, hydration, stress, and sleep. And it was telling me, loudly, that 200 grams in 95-degree heat was not working.

The dehydration angle is what really gets me. Protein metabolism produces urea, which the kidneys excrete in urine. The more protein you eat, the more urea you produce, the more urine you make, the more water you lose. In a heat wave, you're already losing water through sweat. Adding high protein is like running two dehydration protocols simultaneously. I was drinking a gallon of water a day and still waking up with dark urine. Because I was pissing out all the water my body needed to cool itself. The protein was literally stealing my hydration. And I was compensating by drinking more water, which was making my stomach feel even more full and bloated, which was making me drink less during meals, which was making the protein digestion even harder. It was a vicious cycle of my own creation, built on the belief that more protein is always better.

I'm not anti-protein. I'm still a gym rat. I still want to be strong. I still believe in the importance of amino acids for muscle repair and growth. But I've learned that context matters. The number on the label is not the number in your body. The grams you eat are not the grams you absorb. And the environment you're in changes everything. San Diego is not the same city it was ten years ago. The summers are hotter. The heat waves are longer. And the nutritional strategies that worked in mild climates need to be adjusted. Not abandoned. Adjusted. I'm still eating protein. I'm just eating less of it when it's 95 degrees. And I'm eating more carbs, more fruit, more foods that digest easily and don't generate heat. I'm still training. I'm just training smarter. And I'm sleeping better. And I don't feel like I'm going to vomit after every meal. Which, honestly, is a nice change.

So here's my advice, from someone who learned the hard way. If you're in a heat wave, drop your protein target by 20-30%. Drink water with every meal, not just between them. Stop eating protein three hours before bed. Eat more carbs for energy. And listen to your body. If your stomach says no, the answer is no. Not "drink more water and push through it." Not "take a digestive enzyme." Just no. Your body knows what it needs. The problem is that we've been trained to ignore it in favor of numbers on a spreadsheet. And those numbers don't know that it's 95 degrees in your apartment. But you do. And your stomach does. And your sleep does. And your afternoon energy crash does. Trust them. They're telling you the truth.

Anyone else had to dial back the protein in the heat? Or are you still force-feeding chicken breast while the thermometer climbs? Because I'm starting to think the fitness industry needs a summer safety briefing.

The thing that really messed with my head was looking at my training log. I've kept a training log since I was nineteen. Every workout. Every set. Every rep. Every weight. It's a record of my life in iron, and it's one of the things I'm most proud of. I looked at my numbers from June 2026, during the worst of the heat, and I saw something I didn't want to see. My strength was down. Not a lot. But enough. My bench press was off by 10 pounds. My squat was off by 15. My deadlift was the same, but it felt harder. I was working harder for less output. And I was blaming myself. I thought I was getting old. I thought I was losing my edge. I thought maybe I needed a deload week, or a new program, or more protein. I never thought that the heat was stealing my performance. That my body was diverting resources to thermoregulation instead of muscle contraction. That the nausea and bloating were reducing my intra-workout nutrition. That the poor sleep was destroying my recovery. It wasn't me. It was the environment. And I couldn't see it because I was too busy looking at the spreadsheet.

I started talking to other lifters at my gym. The guy who squats in the rack next to me, a powerlifter named Carlos who competes in the 220-pound class, told me he'd been feeling the same way. "I thought I was overtrained," he said. "But I've been sleeping eight hours. I've been deloading. Nothing helped. Then I dropped my protein to 150 grams and added more rice. Three days later, I hit a PR on my squat." Another guy, a bodybuilder who preps for shows, told me he always drops protein and increases carbs two weeks before a competition if the venue is in a hot climate. "The judges don't care about your protein intake," he said. "They care about how you look on stage. And you look better when you're not bloated and nauseous." These were experienced athletes. People who knew their bodies. And they had all figured out what I was just learning: that nutrition is contextual. That the same macros in different environments produce different results. That the heat is not just a background variable. It's a primary driver of how your body processes food.

I started looking at the research on heat and digestion more carefully. The body has a fixed amount of blood. About 5 liters in an adult male. That blood has to go everywhere: the brain, the heart, the muscles, the skin, the digestive system. In a heat wave, the body prioritizes cooling. Blood flow to the skin increases by up to 50% to facilitate sweating and heat dissipation. That blood has to come from somewhere. It comes from the digestive system. It comes from the muscles. It comes from the organs that are not immediately involved in survival. So when you eat a massive protein meal in 95-degree heat, your body doesn't have the blood flow to digest it properly. The food sits in your stomach. It ferments. It causes bloating, nausea, and discomfort. And the protein you think you're absorbing is actually being poorly digested and excreted. You're literally pissing away your gains because your body is too busy staying alive to build muscle.

The dehydration-protein connection is even more insidious. Every gram of protein requires approximately 7 grams of water for the kidneys to process the nitrogenous waste. That's 1,400 grams of water—about 1.4 liters—just to process 200 grams of protein. In a heat wave, when you're already losing 2-3 liters of water per day through sweat, adding another 1.4 liters of water demand is catastrophic. Your body enters a state of chronic low-grade dehydration. Your urine gets dark. Your skin gets dry. Your energy crashes. Your cognitive function declines. And you keep drinking more water to compensate, but you're just running water through a system that's already overloaded. The protein is the bottleneck. And the solution is not more water. It's less protein.

I know this is controversial. I know the fitness industry has built an empire on the idea that protein is the answer to everything. Want to build muscle? Protein. Want to lose fat? Protein. Want to recover faster? Protein. Want to be healthier? Protein. And in many contexts, that's true. But context matters. The context of a heat wave changes the equation. The context of extreme heat changes what your body can process, what it needs, and what will make it sick. And the fitness industry, with its air-conditioned gyms and climate-controlled supplement warehouses, doesn't want to talk about that. Because it complicates the narrative. Because it means that sometimes, less is more. Because it means that the environment you live in is as important as the macros you eat. And that's not a message that sells protein powder.

I'm still a gym rat. I'm still going to hit my protein target when the weather cools down. But I've learned that my body is not a spreadsheet. It's a biological system that exists in a physical environment. And that environment is getting hotter. San Diego is not the mild, temperate paradise it used to be. The heat waves are longer. The temperatures are higher. And the nutritional strategies that worked in the old climate don't work in the new one. I've adapted. I've adjusted. I've dropped my protein, increased my carbs, and started listening to my stomach instead of my calculator. And I'm stronger for it. Not in the gym, necessarily—though my numbers are coming back. But in my daily life. I have energy. I sleep. I don't feel sick. And that's worth more than any PR.

Derek 'Dex' Armstrong
